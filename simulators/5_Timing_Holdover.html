When the satellite clock goes quiet: how long can systems keep time on their own?

Phone masts, power substations and trading computers set their clocks from satellite navigation (GNSS) time. If GNSS is lost, each one falls back on its own internal clock, which slowly drifts.

How to read the bars
The black line is each system's limit: how far its clock may be from true time. The bar grows as the clock drifts and turns red once the limit is broken. Change the clock type in each box to compare.
Why all the clocks drift at once. A space weather disturbance hits every GNSS receiver in a region at the same moment (a common-mode failure). So every system starts running on its own clock together, and nobody can borrow good time from a neighbour.
Each bar runs from the moment GNSS is lost until the limit is broken (typical figure). The thin whisker shows the published range, from a good case to a poor case.

What has actually been reported PUBLISHED
  • San Diego, January 2007. A naval exercise accidentally jammed GPS for about 2 hours. Two mobile operators lost GPS at city-centre masts, switched to other ways of keeping time, and reported no loss of service to customers (US Coast Guard). [20]
  • A mobile operator, case study. GPS jammers in two nearby vans kept putting the operator's timing unit into holdover, for a few minutes to a few hours at a time. It had rubidium backup clocks, so service was not affected. [21]
  • Worldwide, 26 January 2016. Half the GPS satellites broadcast a time correction about 13 µs wrong. Telecoms firms saw about 12 hours of alarms (thousands at some firms) and some digital radio and TV transmitters failed, but no effects were reported that the public would notice, probably thanks to backup clocks. [22]
  • One national mobile network (country and year not given). An old GPS receiver software fault, triggered when a new satellite started broadcasting, crashed the receivers at about 100 sites within minutes. Mobile service failed completely and the network was down for weeks until the receivers were replaced. This was a receiver fault, not space weather, but it shows a common-mode failure. [19]
  • Space weather. I found no documented case of a mobile network failing because of space weather. Solar radio bursts that can make GNSS receivers lose lock last about 20 minutes each. [9, 24]
  • In the UK. 5G in the 3.4–3.8 GHz band must use TDD, with every operator's frames starting at a common reference time. [23] In 2013 the Royal Academy of Engineering found UK mobile networks were not reliant on GPS, but named TDD 4G as a possible exception. [24] I could not find out how each UK operator gets its 5G time today.

Sources: every number in this demo
  1. Phone mast (4G/5G TDD) limit: ±1.5 µs from a common reference. ITU-T Recommendation G.8271 (03/2020), Table 1, Class 4 (LTE-TDD small cell, NR TDD). itu.int
  2. Power substation (grid measurement unit) limit: 31.8 µs at 50 Hz. IEEE C37.118.1 allows 1% total vector error; with no other error, that equals 0.573° of phase, which is 31.8 µs at 50 Hz. The recommended accuracy is 1 µs, so 31.8 µs is the most it could ever tolerate. Goldstein (NIST), ATIS timing workshop 2018. atis.org
  3. Fast (high-frequency) trading limit: 100 µs from UTC; other automated trading 1 ms; voice trading 1 s. UK version of Delegated Regulation 2017/574 (RTS 25), Annex Table 2, kept in the UK FCA Handbook. In the EU it was replaced in March 2026 by Regulation 2025/1155, which keeps the 100 µs limit. legislation.gov.uk, FCA Handbook
  4. Lab-grade reference limit: 100 ns, holdover 14 days (enhanced primary reference time clock). ITU-T G.8272.1. Drawn as a straight line up to 100 ns at 14 days, so it shows the required worst case, not a typical clock. It is not extended beyond 14 days. itu.int
  5. Basic crystal clock (TCXO): drift in the first day. Typical used here: 400 µs (time-server maker's options datasheet A). Good case: 222 µs (chip maker's lab test at steady temperature, application note AN1208; it broke the 1.5 µs limit after 0.36 hours). Poor case: 10 ms (time-server maker's datasheet B). A, lab test, B
  6. Oven-controlled crystal clock (OCXO): first day. Typical: 25 µs (datasheet A). Good case: 0.5 µs (ATIS timing workshop 2018: best crystals 0.5–2 µs in 24 h at steady temperature). Poor case: 80 µs (datasheet B). Cross-check: in the lab test above, an OCXO broke the 1.5 µs limit after 3.4 hours; this demo gives about 2.6 hours. ATIS 2018
  7. Rubidium atomic clock: first day. Typical: 1 µs (datasheet A: under 1 µs in the first 24 hours and under 3 µs after three days). Good case: 0.1 µs (ATIS 2018, best rubidium type, steady temperature). Poor case: 5 µs (datasheet B).
  8. Superstorm GNSS outage: 1–3 days; recommendation that critical systems have holdover for up to three days; UK mobile networks found to be less reliant on GNSS timing than some other countries'. Royal Academy of Engineering (2013), Extreme space weather. raeng.org.uk
  9. Common-mode example: intense solar radio bursts in December 2006 made GPS receivers across the sunlit side of Earth lose tracking at the same time. Cerruti et al. (2008), Space Weather 6, S10D07.
  10. Kp threshold (my data): 50% chance of a disturbed 3-hour block at Kp 7.7 (95% confidence interval 7.4–8.0), Morpeth, 152 blocks over 19 days. Disturbed = more than 2% of satellite windows with ROTI above 0.5 TECU/min.
  11. How the curves are drawn (illustrative). Time error = (first-day figure) × [½ d + ½ d²] for the crystal clocks, where d is days since GNSS was lost (half a steady frequency offset, half ageing), and a straight line for rubidium and the lab reference. Only the one-day values are published; the shape in between is a simplification. It agrees roughly with the lab test times in sources 5 and 6.
  12. Phone mast timing in 4G and 5G (tab 3). Neighbouring TDD cells must start their frames within 3 µs of each other (3GPP TS 38.133, the same as 4G), which is shared out as ±1.5 µs each from a common time. The time arriving at the mast must be within ±1.1 µs, leaving 400 ns for the mast itself. Holdover on the mast's own clock lasts “up to a few hours for a TDD base station”. FDD networks also need time when certain coordination features are used. Ruffini, Johansson, Pohlman and Sandgren (2021), technical review by a network equipment maker. article
  13. Frequency limit: ±0.05 ppm (50 parts per billion) for a wide-area 5G mast (±0.1 ppm for smaller ones). 3GPP TS 38.104, Table 6.5.1.2-1. A network equipment maker's white paper says ±50 ppb applies to 4G and 5G whatever the application, and that missing the TDD timing limit “can result in poor signal quality, lower data speeds, interference and network outages”. 3gpp.org, white paper
  14. Time budget: 1.1 µs network limit for a 1.5 µs mast limit. ITU-T G.8271.1 (2022, amendments 2023–2025). It notes that a long loss of GNSS can break both limits. itu.int
  15. Mast switches its TDD cells off when its clock runs free; FDD carries on. One maker's base station synchronisation guide: “A TDD base station cannot work in free running mode. After the base station enters the free running mode, cells served by the base station are automatically deactivated. These cells are automatically activated only after the base station re-enters the locked mode.” Also: an FDD base station “can work properly in holdover mode for 90 days”, and the holdover time for TDD depends on the clock type. I could read only quoted extracts of this document, not the whole thing. copy of guide
  16. “Must be taken out of service quickly”. A timing-equipment maker's article: 5G radios use low-cost clocks, so a radio with its own GNSS receiver that loses GNSS “must be taken out of service quickly before it begins interfering with adjacent radios”. The radio clocks can hold the 50 ppb frequency limit “for several days”. Masts fed from one shared network clock drift together, which avoids the clash. Olsen (2024), Microwaves & RF. mwrf.com
  17. What users notice when TDD timing slips (measured on 3G TDD). Ji, Liu and Dong (2010), a telecoms equipment maker's journal, vol. 8(2), pp. 43–47: when a TD-SCDMA mast lost GPS, timing differences of more than 4 chips made network quality “deteriorate noticeably” (handover failures, calls dropping during handover, fewer calls connecting), and more than 10 chips meant phones “may fail to locate a neighbouring cell”. One chip lasts 0.78 µs (1.28 million chips per second), so 4 chips ≈ 3.1 µs and 10 chips ≈ 7.8 µs (my conversion). This was 3G; that 4G and 5G behave the same way is argued. abstract
  18. Re-locking takes minutes. One maker's engineering note for older base stations: a returning clock source is watched for 260 s, then a GPS source must stay steady for more than 400 s before the clock starts tracking it. The demo adds these up to 11 minutes, which is illustrative; newer equipment may differ. note
  19. Alarms and holdover in GNSS clocks, and the national network failure. Danielson (2016), GPS incident on broadcast networks, report for the US Civil GPS Service Interface Committee, sections 3.1 and 5.4; Danielson and Silverstein (2016), slides for the US GPS advisory board (“PRN32 launch killed a national telecom network”). report, slides
  20. San Diego GPS jamming, 22 January 2007. About 2 hours of jamming; two mobile operators lost GPS at downtown masts, used other timing, and reported no loss of service (US Coast Guard Navigation Center, quoted in the fact check). GPS World, April 2007; Voice of San Diego fact check, 10 June 2011. GPS World, fact check
  21. Jammers near a mobile operator. A UK timing company's case study (2026): the timing unit went into holdover for “a few minutes to a few hours”; rubidium clocks meant “no real risk of the outages affecting service”. case study
  22. 26 January 2016 GPS time error. 15 satellites broadcast a UTC correction about 13 µs wrong; telecoms clients saw “12 hours” of system errors, with no consequences apparent to the public. BBC News, 4 February 2016; NIST paper on the effects on GPS clocks. BBC, NIST
  23. UK 5G rules. Ofcom licence conditions for the 3.6–3.8 GHz band: TDD frame structure, and “the Licensee shall ensure that frames start at a common reference time so that all licensees' frames are aligned and transmissions synchronised”. The licence does not say where the time must come from. ofcom.org.uk
  24. Royal Academy of Engineering (2013) on mobile networks (source 8, chapter 11): UK 3GPP networks “were specifically designed not to require GPS support”; TDD 4G is “one potential exception”; each solar radio burst lasts about 20 minutes; networks with critical uses should run without GNSS timing for up to three days.
  25. How tab 3 is worked out (illustrative). It uses the same clock drift curves as tabs 1 and 2 (source 11) to find when the error passes 1.5, 3.1 and 7.8 µs. Holdover is assumed to last until GNSS is back and the clock has re-locked (source 18). After re-locking, the error is shown as zero. Common-mode drift between neighbours is argued, not modelled.
