Why it matters. A receiver needs at least 4 healthy satellites for a position. It can drop disturbed ones, but its fix gets worse. Low satellites are often hit first, because their signal takes a longer path through the ionosphere. At Morpeth on 11 May 2024, 24% of readings from low satellites (20–35° up) were rough, against 12% from high ones (above 55°). In the Arctic the rough patch is overhead, so high satellites are hit just as much.
MEASUREDSatellite positions, ROTI (rate of TEC index; TEC = total electron content, the number of electrons along the signal path) and Kp (the 0–9 storm scale, from GFZ Potsdam) are real data from my worldwide station archive: GPS satellites above 20°, 5-minute windows, “rough” = ROTI above 0.5 TECU per minute. Satellite directions were worked out from the broadcast orbit files and agree with the archive's elevations to about 0.1°. Morpeth stands in for Belfast (same magnetic latitude, about 57°). LIMITSOnly GPS is shown; real receivers also use other satellite systems. A gap in a satellite's trail means no reading in that window. “Disturbed” does not mean the signal was lost.
